## What PuzzleScript is

PuzzleScript is a small, open-source scripting language for building two-dimensional, tile-based puzzle games. A game is a single text file with a handful of sections: a list of objects (each with a name, a palette, and an optional 5×5 pixel sprite), a legend mapping single characters to objects or combinations of objects, a set of collision layers that determine which objects can share a cell, a list of rewrite rules, one or more win conditions, and a sequence of levels drawn as blocks of characters. The engine is entirely deterministic: there is no randomness, no real-time element, and no hidden state outside the grid itself. Most games in this family descend from Sokoban, but the rule language is flexible enough to express teleporters, timers, colour mixing, chain reactions, cloned avatars, and many other ideas.

Rules are pattern-matching rewrites. A rule like `[ > Player | Crate ] -> [ > Player | > Crate ]` says "if a player is trying to move into a cell containing a crate, the crate also tries to move in that direction." Rules fire every turn, in order, until nothing changes; rules marked `late` fire after all movement has resolved. A rule can `cancel` a turn, trigger a `restart`, or request an extra automatic turn (`again`). Because rules are small and composable, a game with only five lines of rules can have surprisingly rich behaviour, and a game with forty lines can still be about one simple idea.

Each level is a rectangular grid. On every turn the engine takes one input—one of the four directions or the action button—applies it to every player object simultaneously, runs all the rules, and then checks the win conditions. Win conditions in this corpus are all of the form "all X on Y" or "all Y on X": every crate must sit on a target, every player must stand on a goal, every coloured block must cover a marker of the matching colour, and so on. A level ends the moment all of its win conditions are satisfied simultaneously at the end of a turn.

## How a game is presented to the player

The player interacts through text only. On turn zero they receive a short, fixed preamble that explains the interface and nothing else. It states that the rules are not provided and must be inferred from observation; that the objective is to reach the win condition; that the available actions are `up`, `down`, `left`, `right`, `action`, `undo`, and `restart`; that `action` is a generic button which in some games does nothing; and that the grid is rendered as rows of cells separated by `|`.

Below the preamble is a legend and then the grid. Every cell in the grid is drawn as a fixed-width string of character slots, one character per object currently occupying that cell, with `.` filling unused slots. Because all cells have the same number of slots, the columns line up visually. A cell with a wall and nothing else might read `W|`, while a cell in a more layered game might read `VPCMB` (five objects stacked on one tile) or `EW.` (two objects plus an empty slot). The number of slots per cell is set by the maximum number of objects any cell could hold given the game's collision layers, so it varies from game to game—some games have one slot per cell, others have five.

The legend lists each letter that appears, paired with the game's internal object name in lowercase. These letters are assigned per game and usually, but not always, correspond to the first letter of the object name; when two objects share an initial, one of them is given a different letter. This means the same letter can mean entirely different things in different games, and a player cannot assume `C` is a crate or `G` is a goal without checking. The object names themselves are whatever the game's author typed. Sometimes they are descriptive (`wall`, `goal`, `monster`, `pinkbutton`, `cratespecial`); sometimes they are terse or opaque (`item2`, `item4`, `crate10`, `a`, `b`, `tred`, `tele`); and sometimes they refer to invisible bookkeeping objects the author never meant the player to see (`playermarker`, `boxmarker`, `timermarker9`, `side`, `edge`). Transparent or purely cosmetic objects appear in the text rendering with the same prominence as everything else, and the plain background tile is typically omitted. The legend therefore leaks a partial, noisy description of the game's vocabulary, but it says nothing about what any object does.

After each action the player receives the updated grid. There are no sound effects, no colours, no sprites, no level titles, and no in-game messages: the explanatory text that PuzzleScript authors normally insert between levels ("sponges can wipe up colour", "put the battery in the outlet", "match three crates!") is stripped out. The player is dropped into a single level, not necessarily the first one of its game, and plays only that level. The episode ends when the win condition is met or when a turn budget is exhausted; the player is not told how many turns they have. `undo` reverts one move and `restart` returns the level to its initial state, and both count as turns.

## The games in the corpus

The corpus consists of several distinct games, each contributing one or more levels. They share the Sokoban skeleton—an avatar moves orthogonally, walls block, some things can be pushed—but each adds its own twist, and the twist is what the player has to discover.

**Pushing and placement.** The most basic family: push boxes onto targets, or walk the avatar onto a goal. Even here there are variations. In one game a pushed object slides only in certain orientations—some crates respond only to horizontal shoves, others only to vertical. In another, pushing an object into a particular tile consumes the object entirely, while pushing a different object into a different tile converts it into something else. Blocked pushes simply do nothing; the player stays put and the turn passes.

**Matching and elimination.** One large game is built around a match-three idea: when three identical crates line up orthogonally, all three vanish. The same game layers on batteries that must be filled by pushing coloured bars onto matching slots, keys that are picked up by walking over them and transform the avatar into a key-carrying variant, locks that the key-carrier can open (consuming the key), dumps that destroy whatever is pushed onto them, and dozens of cosmetic wall and crate types that look different but behave identically. Its levels range from a one-row corridor to sprawling mazes.

**Colour mixing.** Another game has coloured blocks that behave like paint: pushing a blue block into a red block fuses them into a single purple block, blue plus green makes cyan, red plus green makes yellow, and several combinations produce white. The win condition demands that every outline marker be covered by a block of exactly the matching colour, so blocks must sometimes be combined and sometimes kept apart.

**Flood fill.** In a floor-painting game, coloured sources spread liquid across the floor every turn—downward first, then outward—until they hit edges, crates, or other liquid. Where two colours meet they blend according to a fixed table, and the fully blended "mix" colour is contagious, overwriting any flood it touches. Ordinary crates dam the flow; a sponge crate absorbs whatever it is pushed into. The avatar's own sprite changes depending on whether it is standing in paint, on bare floor, or on an edge. Goals demand specific colours in specific cells.

**Multiple avatars.** One game places several player objects on the board at once, often in separate rooms. Every input moves all of them together; one may be blocked by a wall while another is free, and this desynchronisation is the whole puzzle. Every target must end up under a player.

**Buttons, doors, and grates.** A game about escaping a maze uses heavy stones that can be pushed in chains but cannot cross grated floor or pass through doors. Colour-coded buttons, when weighed down by a stone, make the matching door vanish; lift the stone and the door returns. Only the avatar needs to reach the goal.

**Teleporters and hazards.** A small cave game has blue teleporter tiles: stepping onto one while moving in some direction projects the mover along that direction to the next non-solid cell, and the same applies to pushed crates. Monsters are stationary but lethal—standing adjacent to one marks the avatar dead and the level restarts automatically on the following input.

**Timers and loops.** A time-loop game displays a countdown digit in a corner of the grid. Each turn the digit decrements; when it reaches zero the level resets—the avatar and every box snap back to their starting cells and the timer resets—but pickups the avatar has already collected (one grants the ability to destroy boxes, another a double-step speed) remain in effect, visibly appended to the wall of the top-right corner. Special floor tiles extend the timer's future value. The goal is a green cell the avatar must occupy before the countdown runs out.

**Linked motion.** A game about floating islands has a special crate that, when pushed, drags a magic field (and a slab of moveable ground beneath it) along the line of an invisible beam, letting the player reposition patches of walkable floor over a void. Stepping onto void is simply refused. Ordinary crates must still end up on targets.

## Level selection and scale

The levels chosen from these games are generally short: the optimal solutions in this collection run from about five to twelve moves. Grids range from a tiny 3×7 strip to a 14×16 room, and a few games in the corpus contain much larger levels than those sampled. Rule counts range from a single pushing rule to nearly forty rules handling timers, markers, and resets. Levels are drawn from the middle of a game's progression as often as from its start, so the player may face a level that assumes familiarity with a mechanic introduced three levels earlier, without ever having seen that introduction.

Because play is blind, the same level is both a puzzle and an experiment: the player cannot read the rules, cannot see the win condition, and receives no text beyond the legend and the grid. What the player does get is perfect, immediate, deterministic feedback after every action, and the ability to undo or restart at will. Every change in the grid between one turn and the next is the direct consequence of the chosen input passing through the game's rules, so the full behaviour of the level is in principle recoverable from observation alone—within the turn budget.
